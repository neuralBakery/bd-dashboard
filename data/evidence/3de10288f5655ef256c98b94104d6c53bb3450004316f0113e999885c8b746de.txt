<p>C4C is a federally funded initiative that aims to strengthen the health and wellbeing of children aged five to 12 and their families in areas of recognised disadvantage, with programs and resources to help them flourish and lead happy, healthy lives.</p>
<p>Programs include free playgroups and community events, as well as resources to support workers in the early childhood sector.</p>
<p>In partnership with The Y Bendigo, the local branch of the organisation managed by Anglicare Victoria worked with the kids to also record how they want to change the world.</p>
<p>C4C Bendigo project manager Jude Di Manno said C4C&#8217;s own 21st birthday felt like a milestone worth celebrating through the recording.</p>
<p>&#8220;We know one of the best ways to empower children is by listening to them, and our latest project was an opportunity for them to speak up about what they want and need from the world around them to thrive,&#8221; she said.</p>
<p>Di Manno said one of the clearest messages was a gentle reminder for adults to put aside distractions and be fully present when talking with them.</p>
<p>Asked how they knew a grown-up was really listening, they said it was when adults looked at them and were not on a phone, tablet or computer, playing a game or watching TV.</p>
<p>&#8220;Because I can feel it in my heart, it feels like happiness,&#8221; one participant said.</p>
<p>The full audio project is available for viewing on the C4C Bendigo YouTube channel.</p>
<p>C4C Bendigo is delivered in partnership with Anglicare Victoria, Baptistcare, the Bendigo and District Aboriginal Co-operative, Noah&#8217;s Ark and Shine Bright.</p>
<p>For more information, visit <a href="http://c4cbendigo.com.au" target="_blank" rel="noopener">c4cbendigo.com.au</a></p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<img width="640" height="336" src="https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/North-Images-041126-13-1024x538.jpg" class="webfeedsFeaturedVisual wp-post-image" alt="Anne Conway Phoenix FM" style="display: block; margin-bottom: 10px; clear: both; max-width: 100%;" decoding="async" loading="lazy" srcset="https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/North-Images-041126-13-1024x538.jpg 1024w, https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/North-Images-041126-13-300x158.jpg 300w, https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/North-Images-041126-13-768x403.jpg 768w, https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/North-Images-041126-13.jpg 1200w" sizes="auto, (max-width: 640px) 100vw, 640px" /><p>Phoenix FM founding member and volunteer Anne Conway OAM has been inducted into the Community Broadcasting Association of Australia&#8217;s Honour Roll.</p>
<p>The register recognises those who have given more than 15 years&#8217; service to a community broadcaster, and Conway was one of nine people admitted at the association&#8217;s awards ceremony in Geelong.</p>
<p>During the ceremony&#8217;s opening address, Phoenix was also recognised for assisting in airing ABC coverage during January&#8217;s Harcourt and Mount Alexander fires, with departed station volunteers Bradley &#8220;Monkey&#8221; Hopgood and Richard Jones also remembered in a tribute to passed community radio identities.</p>
<p>Conway said she was &#8220;still a little overwhelmed&#8221; and very grateful to have even been nominated in the first place, remarking that her work over the years had not been &#8220;entirely selfless&#8221;.</p>
<p>&#8220;I get a lot back by helping other people,&#8221; she said.&#8221;I think it&#8217;s just something I was raised to do.&#8221;</p>
<p>She went on to dedicate her latest honour to her fellow founding members.</p>
<p>&#8220;Without them I wouldn&#8217;t have the award,&#8221; she said.</p>
<p>The founding group first met in late 2006 and after much lobbying the station made its first transmission in January 2008.</p>
<p>Obtaining its broadcasting licence was &#8220;very difficult&#8221; according to Conway, but the station had secured it in record time and subsequently established itself as a &#8220;true access&#8221; programmer with &#8220;something for absolutely everyone&#8221;.</p>
<p>Phoenix FM also won two annual awards on the night &#8211; Excellence in Australian Music and Outstanding Presenter, the latter of which went to Brett Adie.</p>
<p>Adie was praised for his work on programs including Live Music Bendigo, nationally syndicated Aussie Trove, and The Phatt, as well as for helping out with Phoenix&#8217;s intermittent external broadcasts.</p>
<p>In terms of Phoenix&#8217;s Aussie music award, Conway &#8211; a former country music performer, songwriter, promoter and Tamworth regular &#8211; explained that under the Broadcasting Services Act 1992 (Part 5) the station was required to play 25 per cent Australian music, but had always &#8220;punched above that&#8221;.</p>
<p>&#8220;Not so much now, but once upon a time it was very hard for Australian artists to get any airplay,&#8221; she said.</p>
<p>&#8220;There was this opinion that the good music was English or American, so the Australian artists had to go overseas.</p>
<p>&#8220;That&#8217;s what The Seekers did, then you had Olivia Newton-John.</p>
<p>&#8220;That&#8217;s where community radio comes into its own.</p>
<p>&#8220;Unlike commercial radio we choose our own playlists, who we want to play.</p>
<p>&#8220;Even if no one&#8217;s ever heard of them, if we like them, local independent artists are now getting a fair go.&#8221;</p>
<p>Conway also took the opportunity to urge local businesses to consider sponsoring the station as &#8220;a cost-effective way of getting your message out into the community&#8221;.</p>
<p>Find Phoenix FM at 106.7 on the FM dial or online, with more than 20 programs available on demand at <a href="http://phoenixfm.org.au" target="_blank" rel="noopener">phoenixfm.org.au</a></p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<img width="640" height="336" src="https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/tng-04-nov-32-1024x538.jpg" class="webfeedsFeaturedVisual wp-post-image" alt="Steven Stanley abstract artist" style="display: block; margin-bottom: 10px; clear: both; max-width: 100%;" decoding="async" loading="lazy" srcset="https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/tng-04-nov-32-1024x538.jpg 1024w, https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/tng-04-nov-32-300x158.jpg 300w, https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/tng-04-nov-32-768x403.jpg 768w, https://static.timesnewsgroup.com.au/prod/uploads/sites/24/2026/11/tng-04-nov-32.jpg 1200w" sizes="auto, (max-width: 640px) 100vw, 640px" /><p>This week we chat with local abstract realist painter Steven Stanley, who said being a regional artist can come with challenges, especially when it comes to access to galleries, exhibitions and networks.</p>
<p><strong>What motivated you to become a painter?</strong></p>
<p>I&#8217;ve been drawn to art for as long as I can remember.</p>
<p>There&#8217;s always been something about creating that has made sense to me, even when other things haven&#8217;t.</p>
<p><strong>How would you describe your painting style?</strong></p>
<p>Abstract realism, with a strong focus on emotion, colour and the relationship between the two.</p>
<p>I like combining recognisable subjects, particularly faces, birds and elements of nature, with more abstract, expressive marks.</p>
<p>I use a combination of acrylics, oils, inks, pastels and collage, and I&#8217;m always looking for new ways to create depth and texture.</p>
<p><strong>Which other painters inspire you?</strong></p>
<p>I absolutely love the way Christian Hook paints.</p>
<p>It’s a beautiful balance between realism and abstraction that feels so right.</p>
<p>Tahlia Stanton’s work embodies a sense of playfulness and colour in such a beautiful way that I absolutely adore.</p>
<p>As I was growing up, the impressionists were my absolute favourites.</p>
<p>I also really love the pre-Raphaelites for their love of nature and beauty.</p>
<p><strong>Have you had any mentors over your career?</strong></p>
<p>I studied with Joy Tatt for 5 years.</p>
<p>She was able to make me see colour in things that I hadn’t noticed before.</p>
<p>I’ve also attended one of Alanah Ellen Brand’s portrait workshops, where she was able to teach me how to simplify the painting process.</p>
<p><strong>What are some highlights from your time following your pursuits so far?</strong></p>
<p>Attending art classes with Joy has proved to be more than just classes, more a great friendship and with other students too.</p>
<p>I’ve had three solo exhibitions in the past two years and seeing people’s (reactions) has been priceless.</p>
<p>More recently, I&#8217;ve started exploring ways to bring my art into people&#8217;s lives beyond the traditional painting on a wall.</p>
<p>The biggest highlight, though, is the ongoing evolution.</p>
<p><strong>How can people check out your work online?</strong></p>
<p>My website is www.artbystevenstanley.com.au, where people can explore my artwork and find out more about what I do.</p>
<p>I&#8217;m on Instagram at @artbystevenstanley.</p>
<p><strong>What are you working on at the moment? </strong></p>
<p>I&#8217;m continuing to develop my painting practice while exploring new ways to connect with people through creativity.</p>
<p>I&#8217;m also developing ideas around painting workshops and an online mixed-media painting course.</p>
<p>Alongside those projects, I&#8217;m continuing to make and share my own artwork, explore new techniques and build my career.</p>
<p><strong>Where do you see yourself with your pursuits in a couple of years? What about in 10 years?</strong></p>
<p>I&#8217;d love to have a more established presence as an artist.</p>
<p>I&#8217;d also like to have my creative projects, workshops and online courses operating in a way that allows me to spend more time doing what I love: creating art and connecting with people.</p>
<p>In ten years, I&#8217;d love to have built a sustainable creative career.</p>
<p><strong>What would you say to young regional aspiring painters who are thinking of making a go of the creative life?</strong></p>
<p>Give it a go, but understand that it&#8217;s a journey.</p>
<p>Being a regional artist can come with challenges, especially when it comes to access to galleries, exhibitions and networks.</p>
<p>But, you can also build connections online, develop your audience and find ways to get your work in front of people.</p>
<p>Learn the business side of things too.</p>
<p>Most importantly, make work that means something to you.</p>

It needs only a pair of scissors and the political resolve to mislead.</p>
<p>Victorian Labor is running a television advertisement in which the Opposition Leader, Jess Wilson, is asked whether Americanising our healthcare system would be a bad thing. She replies: &#8220;Not at all.&#8221; The words are hers. The interpretation is Labor’s.</p>
<p>The Age asked the question: would Ms. Wilson’s proposed policy &#8220;Americanise&#8221; the state&#8217;s health system, and was that bad? The Liberal leader’s answer — “not at all” — was not an endorsement of an American style health system. It was the opposite — a complete and flat rejection: “This is happening in Queensland today. In fact, the Labor government has implemented it here in the past &#8230; We need to be partnering &#8230; our private hospitals with the public system to make sure that Victorians get surgery sooner.&#8221;</p>
<p>Sensing voter approval, Labor strategists employed an infamous, grubby technique. They deliberately edited her words, knowing it to be agitprop — “agitation and propaganda” — a Soviet Union tactic for alarming the masses with messages unfettered by fact.</p>
<p>The Coalition has promised $1.3 billion for 25,000 extra annual surgeries — admitting public patients into private hospitals at the state&#8217;s expense. One may debate the merits and costings; however, pretending a public patient having a hip replaced in a private hospital — without paying a cent — is the slippery slope to requiring patients produce a credit card before the health system will provide, is a premeditated, cynical lie.</p>
<p>Constituents should not be subjected to a litany of lies and half truths by those asking us to entrust them with governing the state. The Labor Premier, Ben Carroll, espouses trust, fairness, and Victorian values. That is the shame.</p>
<p>Health care is crucial. It is one of the Nation’s greatest concerns. In 2016 federal election, Labor ran the Mediscare campaign. This lie is the same old nag hauling a different cart. It was a shameful tactic which worked. Public health is the ultimate platform for misnomers and political duplicity; it frightens the elderly and the chronically ill — those most reliant on the system. It is premeditated malice — political sleight of hand masquerading as concern.</p>
<p>Nor is this a policy dispute — it is intended scaremongering — created to instil fear in the vulnerable community.
